## The Political Brain

Westen's thesis: voters decide on feelings, not on a dispassionate weighing of
policies, and Republicans write for that while Democrats lecture.

### Reason is not the engine

- Bounded rationality (the availability bias) explains why a Pennsylvania coal
  miner overweights terrorism after September 11. It doesn't explain why
  terrorism was more available to him than to a New Yorker who had more to
  fear, or why gay marriage in San Francisco loomed larger than nearby mine
  disasters.

- Partisanship trumps evidence. In Bush v. Gore the justices split along party
  lines, while Clarence Thomas told students that the "rules of the political
  world" do not apply to the Court. Dershowitz: the only decision he knew of
  where "the majority justices decided as they did because of the personal
  identity and political affiliation of the litigants."

### Evidence that emotion moves votes

- Allentown, 1930s: a Socialist candidate's field experiment. A week before
  the election, one ward got an emotional leaflet, one a rational leaflet, and
  one nothing. Socialist votes rose by 50 percent, about a third, and about a
  quarter respectively. (One campaign, three wards.)

<img src = "../src/80_westen_political_brain/allentown_emotional_leaflet_experiment.jpg" width = 500px>

### Campaigns, good and bad

- Dukakis, 1988 debate, asked whether he would favor the death penalty if his
  wife were raped and murdered, answered that he saw no evidence the death penalty deters. Westen:
  the election was over in that first minute.

<img src = "../src/80_westen_political_brain/dukakis_kitty_death_penalty_debate.jpg" width = 500px>

- The Willie Horton ad was built for the amygdala: unflattering photos of
  Dukakis, a mug shot of a black man, and every spoken word paired with a
  threatening image. Horton never went by "Willie."

### What to do

- The message grid: what will I say about me, about my opponent; what will my
  opponent say about himself, about me.

<img src = "../src/80_westen_political_brain/message_grid_courage_under_fire.jpg" width = 500px>

- A party's story should have protagonists and antagonists, a clear moral,
  vivid and visualizable elements, and metaphor; it should take over the
  opposition's metaphors; and it should be one its authors would tell their
  children.

<img src = "../src/80_westen_political_brain/p147_political_narrative_criteria.jpg" width = 500px>

- Four principles for messages:
  1. If you don't feel it, don't use it. Decide what voters should feel.
  2. Frame for emotional impact (after Lakoff). Calling homosexuality a
     "lifestyle" makes it a choice, and so open to moral condemnation.
  3. Pitch at Rosch's "basic level": "that woman in the blue coat," not "that
     vertebrate in brightly colored apparel."
  4. Appeal to the whole brain: images and sounds, not just words. Hence the
     Bush administration kept soldiers' caskets out of sight; video from
     Mogadishu ended the Somalia intervention, while casualty counts from Iraq
     moved little.
